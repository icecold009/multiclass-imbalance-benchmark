\begin{abstract}
How should sampler performance be compared when some conditions are inapplicable or fail? We compared fixed imbalance-handling conditions on 11 public multiclass tabular datasets (9 numeric, 2 mixed) using logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost. Preprocessing was fold-local, and evaluation used stratified five-fold cross-validation with three seeds. Macro-F1 was the sole primary metric. The protocol was frozen internally, not publicly preregistered.

Of 5,445 enumerated records, 1,005 were unsupported; of 4,440 applicable attempts, 4,191 were valid and 249 failed in the sampler. Four of 32 planned asymptotic Friedman tests were significant (each $p=0.002292$), all among four outcomes on one numeric XGBoost block of three exactly balanced datasets. For macro-F1, seven of nine conditions tied exactly with raw. SMOTEENN and SMOTETomek had opposite mean-rank extremes (9 and 1; planned Nemenyi $p=0.010464$). Post hoc exact paired sign flips on these three blocks cannot attain a two-sided $p$ below 0.25.

A post hoc grouped-profile sensitivity changed the split unit on six datasets and yielded a mean grouped-minus-row-wise macro-F1 change of $-0.0046$ across 153 matched cells. It addresses generalization to unseen predictor profiles, not identified entities. These results show that applicability, failures, complete-cell coverage, and split design qualify performance summaries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Performance comparisons in multiclass tabular learning become difficult to interpret when a sampler is inapplicable to one feature type, fails on some datasets, or contributes to fewer complete dataset cells than its alternatives. A mean score alone does not show whether methods were compared on the same datasets or whether missing results were unsupported or failed. These coverage differences matter even when preprocessing is leakage-safe.

CLIMB and TILBench already provide broad empirical benchmarks of imbalanced tabular learning. CLIMB covers 73 datasets and 29 methods; TILBench evaluates more than 40 methods across 57 datasets, including 23 multiclass tasks \citep{liu2025climb,liu2026tilbenchv2}. We address a different accounting question within a fixed multiclass comparison: how feature-type-specific applicability, sampler failures, and unequal complete-cell coverage qualify performance summaries.

We compare 11 public datasets (9 numeric, 2 mixed) with logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost. The numeric and mixed cores contain 9 and 7 conditions, respectively. We report the condition matrix as unsupported, valid, failed, or complete; summarize dataset-level paired effects under the declared complete-cell rules; and include post-sampling row counts and runtime. This study evaluates fixed comparators rather than proposing a new sampling method. The scope, settings, metrics, and complete-case rules were frozen internally before the full run and analysis; there was no public or independent timestamped preregistration.

We interpret results within the retained datasets, classifiers, conditions, and metrics, and report row-count and runtime costs alongside performance.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Imbalance interventions.}
Reviews describe established imbalance-handling families that include class weighting, over- and under-sampling, synthetic generation, and hybrid cleaning methods \citep{he2009learning,branco2016survey}. SMOTE generates synthetic minority examples by interpolating between minority neighbours \citep{chawla2002smote}; ADASYN adapts the amount of synthesis to local learning difficulty \citep{he2008adasyn}; and Borderline-SMOTE concentrates synthesis near difficult class boundaries \citep{han2005borderline}. Random over- and under-sampling provide simpler distributional changes. SMOTEENN and SMOTETomek combine over-sampling with cleaning or Tomek-link removal, respectively, and are representative of combination approaches studied in the imbalance literature \citep{batista2004study}. Our benchmark treats these methods as locked comparators, not as tunable algorithmic contributions.

\paragraph{Models and metrics.}
The model set intentionally spans a linear classifier, a bagged tree ensemble, and gradient-boosted trees. Balanced Random Forest is included as an RF-specific comparator following the idea of drawing balanced bootstrap samples for trees \citep{chen2004using}. XGBoost is a widely used gradient-boosting implementation for tabular prediction \citep{chen2016xgboost}. We use macro-F1 as the primary metric and report multiclass G-mean, Matthews correlation coefficient, balanced accuracy, and per-class recall as secondary outcomes. This combination reflects the need to evaluate both class-wise performance and agreement beyond overall accuracy \citep{sokolova2009systematic,gorodkin2004comparing}.

\paragraph{Benchmarking and reproducibility.}
CLIMB and TILBench provide broader benchmarks with more datasets and methods than the present study. Both fit preprocessing and resampling on training data. Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-comparison} summarizes their designs and how they relate to this comparison. Here, we report an explicit coverage account for a fixed multiclass matrix, including unsupported combinations, sampler failures, and complete dataset-level cells. The sampling operators come from the open-source imbalanced-learn ecosystem \citep{lemaitre2017imbalanced}.

\section{Benchmark Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Datasets and scope}

The benchmark was scoped to independent, public, multiclass tabular datasets with at least three classes, documented source terms, and sufficient support for five-fold stratification under a pre-run feasibility and scope audit (internally called Stage 0). The candidate registry contained 25 OpenML/UCI records. Eligibility and feature-type decisions were made from provenance, target, leakage, duplicate, missingness, and support audits; preliminary scores were not used to retain or remove datasets. Eleven datasets were retained: nine numeric datasets and two mixed datasets (CMC and Dermatology). Balanced and near-balanced tasks were not excluded by the scope rule: the exact-balanced CNAE-9, Iris, and Seeds datasets and, for example, Optdigits (IR 1.03) and Wine (IR 1.48) meet the multiclass and support criteria and were retained independently of observed performance. Multiple Features (Factors) was deferred because its corrected replay took 1,043.5 seconds against the 900-second pre-run feasibility budget. This is a computational scope decision, not a score-based exclusion.

Table~\ref{tab:datasets} reports the retained registry fields used in the analysis. The imbalance ratio is the majority-class count divided by the minority-class count. The retained collection spans 150--5,620 rows, 3--10 classes, 4--856 raw predictors, and imbalance ratios from 1.0 to 92.6. Minimum class support ranges from 5 to 554, making the complete-cell and failure rules important for interpreting the result coverage.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

Each dataset uses stratified five-fold cross-validation with shuffling and seeds $0$, $1$, and $2$. A required record is identified by dataset, classifier, condition, seed, and fold, yielding 15 seed-fold records per cell. Rows with missing targets are removed before splitting. Predictor imputation, encoding, scaling, resampling, and training-fold weights are fitted within each training fold. The held-out fold is only transformed and predicted; it is never resampled or used to fit a preprocessing operation.

Numeric pipelines impute numeric values with the training median and scale after imputation. Mixed pipelines impute numeric values with the training median and categorical values with the training mode, ordinal-encode categorical predictors before SMOTENC, and one-hot encode after resampling with unknown categories ignored. Numeric SMOTEENN and SMOTETomek use SMOTE internally; their mixed-data counterparts use SMOTENC internally. The mixed cleaning stages receive ordinal-coded categories, so their neighbour distances can impose an artificial ordering on category codes. Semantic categorical overrides are recorded separately from the registry so that numeric source codes are not treated as continuous by accident. This implementation detail limits how literally the mixed-hybrid results should be interpreted.

\subsection{Models and conditions}

The classifiers are logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost. Logistic regression uses a maximum of 1,000 iterations. Random forest uses 200 trees and one worker. XGBoost uses 200 estimators, the histogram tree method, multiclass log loss for evaluation, and one worker. The locked conditions are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:conditions}; sampler parameters are deliberately simple and fixed rather than tuned after seeing the full-run scores.

\subsection{Metrics and aggregation}

Macro-F1 is the primary outcome, computed over all declared target classes with zero division set to zero. Secondary outcomes are multiclass G-mean, MCC, balanced accuracy, and per-class recall. If $R_c$ denotes recall for class $c$, then

\begin{equation}
G = \left(\prod_{c=1}^{C} R_c\right)^{1/C}.
\label{eq:gmean}
\end{equation}

All encoded classes are included in Equation~\ref{eq:gmean}; a zero class recall makes G-mean exactly zero. MCC is the multiclass Matthews coefficient, balanced accuracy is the unweighted mean recall, and per-class recall uses the same declared labels and zero behavior.

Our G-mean is the geometric mean of per-class recall. TILBench defines G-mean as $\sqrt{\bar{R}\bar{S}}$, where $\bar{R}$ and $\bar{S}$ are macro-averaged sensitivity and specificity; its reported values measure a different quantity and are not numerically comparable \citep{liu2026tilbenchv2}.

Metric values are first averaged over the 15 seed-fold records within a complete dataset--classifier--condition cell. A cell is complete only if all 15 records are valid. The statistical block is the dataset, not the fold or seed. Planned Friedman tests and their Nemenyi follow-ups use only datasets with a complete cell for every condition in the family and selected classifier. The implementation formed each raw-versus-alternative comparison from complete cells for raw and that alternative only, so the dataset count can vary by comparison. For numeric XGBoost, most raw-baseline comparisons use nine dataset blocks, while ADASYN uses three. This pair-specific rule departs from the frozen protocol's family-wide complete-case rule. We therefore report raw-baseline effect estimates, intervals, and tests as exploratory, not as planned confirmatory evidence. A Wilcoxon test was computed only when at least three paired dataset blocks were complete; comparisons with fewer blocks are descriptive.

\subsection{Statistics and efficiency}

For every non-baseline condition, the paired difference is the condition mean minus the raw-baseline mean for the same dataset, classifier, and metric. We report mean and median paired differences, win/tie/loss counts, paired rank-biserial correlation, and a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval with 10,000 resamples and NumPy generator seed 0. Win/tie/loss uses the locked $10^{-12}$ tolerance, whereas Wilcoxon removes only exact-zero differences; a round-off-sized nonzero delta can therefore count as a descriptive tie while remaining in a test. Supplementary \texttt{wilcoxon\_effects.csv} reports the effective nonzero count and minimum attainable $p$ when the actual signed-rank calibration is exact. Bootstrap intervals are unadjusted, use dataset blocks, and are separate from the Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon procedure; they are descriptive with fewer than three blocks.

The frozen analysis plan uses two-sided tests with $\alpha=0.05$. Friedman tests \citep{friedman1937use} compare all conditions on family-wide complete blocks when at least three dataset blocks and three conditions are available. Significant Friedman tests are followed by the planned all-pairs Nemenyi procedure \citep{nemenyi1963distribution} on the same blocks. The implementation also computed two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests \citep{wilcoxon1945individual} for raw versus each alternative using pair-specific complete blocks, with Holm correction within each family, classifier, and metric. Because this pairwise rule departs from the frozen family-wide rule, these Wilcoxon and Holm results are exploratory. Eligible all-zero comparisons receive $p=1$ and remain in their Holm family; rows with fewer than three complete pairs receive no test or adjusted $p$-value. Macro-F1 is the sole primary outcome; G-mean, MCC, and balanced accuracy are supporting secondary outcomes. Post hoc sensitivities include seeded within-block rank permutations and exact paired signed-rank sign flips, with their exchangeability assumptions stated alongside the results.

Fit-and-predict elapsed time, pre- and post-prediction process RSS snapshots and their difference, and training rows before and after sampling are descriptive efficiency outcomes. No peak-memory measurement was collected. Imbalance ratio, class count, raw and encoded dimensionality, and feature type are exploratory moderators. With eleven datasets, moderator patterns are descriptive and cannot establish a selection rule.

\subsection{Reproducibility boundary}

The source package freezes the configuration, registry, acquisition manifest, environment record, scope lock, raw-run manifest, analysis manifest, and local analysis replay and validation report (internally called Gate E). The frozen analysis manifest records Python 3.13.12, but the full-run manifest omits its interpreter; the original benchmark training runtime is therefore unverified. The generated raw and analysis results remain ignored by Git but are hashed in the local release payload. The executable scripts and tests preserve the fold boundaries, validity accounting, aggregation, statistical rules, and figure generation. The paper is a draft for review; it does not imply external submission or venue acceptance.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Coverage and failures}

The full run enumerated 5,445 records. The applicability audit classifies 1,005 as unsupported combinations, leaving 4,440 applicable attempted evaluations: 4,191 valid records and 249 sampler-specific failures, with no other applicable failures. The sampler-specific failure rate is 5.61\% of applicable attempts. The analysis contains 278 complete dataset--classifier--condition cells. Complete-cell counts were 75, 84, and 75 for logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost in the numeric family, respectively; the corresponding mixed-family counts were 14, 16, and 14. The additional RF counts reflect the Balanced Random Forest comparator. These counts are not interchangeable with the number of folds: every complete cell contains 15 seed-fold records.

The 1,005 unsupported records comprise 405 numeric SMOTENC rows, 330 Balanced Random Forest rows outside the RF classifier, and 270 ordinary SMOTE, ADASYN, or Borderline-SMOTE rows on mixed data. The 249 sampler-specific failures are all numeric ADASYN: 207 reported that the requested ratio generated no samples and 42 reported no majority neighbours. These are distinct from unsupported combinations and remain visible in the raw failure log and failure-accounting table; neither category is scored as a model outcome.

\subsection{Planned inference}

There were 32 Friedman rows, four of which were significant. All four came from the same numeric-core XGBoost block on CNAE-9, Iris, and Seeds, each with IR 1.00 and nine conditions. Macro-F1, G-mean, MCC, and balanced accuracy each had statistic 24.0 and asymptotic $p=0.002292$. These correlated outcomes on the same three datasets are not four independent confirmations; macro-F1 was the sole primary metric.

For each of these four metrics, seven of the nine conditions (raw plus class weighting, random over- and under-sampling, SMOTE, ADASYN, and Borderline-SMOTE) tied exactly with raw on every block. SMOTEENN and SMOTETomek had opposite mean-rank extremes, 9.0 and 1.0, respectively. Their planned Nemenyi comparison was the only $p<0.05$ pair ($p=0.010464$). This result distinguishes the two cleaning pipelines on balanced datasets; it is not evidence of improved minority-class rebalancing. The exact-tie pattern is specific to XGBoost: on the same balanced cells, random-forest class weighting and random undersampling had nonzero macro-F1 differences from raw.

\subsection{Exploratory raw-baseline comparisons}

The raw-baseline table contains 184 rows (46 per metric): 24 numeric-core, 18 mixed-core, two numeric Balanced-RF, and two mixed Balanced-RF comparisons. Of these, 104 rows have at least three pair-specific complete blocks and received Wilcoxon tests and Holm correction; 80 rows with one or two blocks are descriptive. Because this pair-specific rule departs from the frozen family-wide complete-case rule, all 104 tests and adjusted values are exploratory, not planned confirmatory evidence. Twelve eligible all-zero ADASYN comparisons have $p=1$ and remain in their eight-test Holm families. The supplementary table reports each test's effective nonzero count and, where exact calibration applies, its minimum attainable $p$.

One adjusted value was below 0.05: numeric logistic regression/MCC with SMOTEENN had nine nonzero differences (nine losses), mean delta $-0.0667$, median delta $-0.0265$, rank-biserial correlation $-1.0$, exact two-sided $p=0.003906$ (the minimum attainable), and Holm-adjusted $p=0.03125$. Its unadjusted 95\% paired-block bootstrap interval was $[-0.1105,-0.0283]$. The bootstrap interval is distinct from the Holm test. This exploratory result is neither confirmatory nor a sampler recommendation.

For mixed XGBoost/balanced accuracy/SMOTENC, the pairwise deltas are $+0.007018053$ (CMC) and $-2.22\times10^{-16}$ (Dermatology). The locked $10^{-12}$ win/tie/loss tolerance gives one win and one tie, while exact-zero Wilcoxon preprocessing retains both nonzero values. With only two complete pairs, the comparison is descriptive and no Wilcoxon test is run; the declared tolerance and zero rules are unchanged.

\subsection{Post hoc sensitivities}

The original three-block XGBoost macro-F1 rank-permutation sensitivity used 200,000 seeded permutations and returned 23 exceedances, $p=0.000120$ (Monte Carlo SE $0.000024$). Exact two-sided sign flips enumerate only eight patterns per paired contrast; the minimum attainable $p$ is $2/8=0.25$, so this test cannot reject at 0.05 even before Holm correction. This resolution limit does not contradict the planned asymptotic result. No pair survived Holm correction (smallest adjusted $p=1.0$). The omnibus permutation assumes condition-label exchangeability within each selected dataset under its null; paired sign flips assume independent sign exchangeability of dataset-level differences. These post hoc sensitivities do not replace planned asymptotic inference. The other 28 Friedman rows were non-significant or skipped; mixed-family tests had only two complete blocks and were skipped under the three-block rule.

Excluding ADASYN leaves eight numeric conditions across the same nine numeric datasets for each classifier. The asymptotic Friedman statistics and seeded permutation $p$-values were 20.511 and 0.001845 for logistic regression, 13.749 and 0.047595 for random forest, and 16.466 and 0.014400 for XGBoost; the corresponding asymptotic $p$-values were 0.004566, 0.055831, and 0.021183. Classifier-specific permutation sensitivities are not adjusted across classifiers. Exact signed-rank sign flips for the seven raw-baseline follow-ups per classifier, with Holm correction within each seven-test family, produced no adjusted $p<0.05$ (the smallest was 0.136719 for XGBoost/SMOTEENN). These analyses change both the condition set and dataset coverage; they do not isolate a causal explanation, and non-significance does not establish equivalence.

The descriptive post hoc numeric stratum at IR 1.00 contains CNAE-9, Iris, and Seeds (3 datasets); the IR $>1.00$ stratum contains Balance Scale, Glass, Optdigits, Wine, Wine Quality Red, and Yeast (6). The supplementary table reports each classifier-condition mean and median paired macro-F1 delta for these strata. No inference is drawn from the three-dataset balanced stratum, and the other six datasets do not form a homogeneous imbalance regime.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\subsection{Efficiency costs}

Sampling changed the number of training rows in the expected direction, but the magnitude depended strongly on the dataset. Across raw numeric cells, the average training set size before sampling was 992 rows. SMOTE increased this to an average of 1,548 rows, random over-sampling had the same average post-sampling size, SMOTETomek averaged 1,518 rows, and SMOTEENN averaged 1,247 rows; random under-sampling reduced it to 661 rows. These are descriptive averages over dataset-level efficiency rows, not a claim that every dataset follows the same pattern.

The average fit-and-predict time for numeric random forest was 0.474 seconds for the raw baseline and 0.625 seconds for SMOTETomek; Balanced Random Forest averaged 0.721 seconds. For numeric XGBoost, the raw baseline averaged 0.538 seconds, while SMOTETomek averaged 0.737 seconds. The numeric ADASYN mean for XGBoost was 0.860 seconds, again based on only three complete datasets. In the two mixed datasets, RF averaged 0.365 seconds for the raw baseline, 0.501 seconds for SMOTENC, and 0.538 seconds for Balanced Random Forest. The timer starts immediately before the pre-fit RSS sample and stops immediately after prediction; it includes that RSS read, pipeline construction, fit-argument preparation, fitting, and prediction, but excludes metric calculation and the post-fit RSS read. The memory values are endpoint process-RSS snapshots and their difference, not peak memory. These measurements support treating computational cost and applicability as first-class benchmark outcomes.

\subsection{Post hoc grouped-profile sensitivity}

A separate post hoc sensitivity used grouped five-fold splits on the six datasets with observed row-wise predictor-profile overlap, retaining all rows and recorded targets. All 90 planned splits were feasible, contained every class in train and test, and had zero profile overlap. The grouped-minus-row-wise macro-F1 difference averaged $-0.0046$ across 153 complete matched condition cells (median $-0.0015$; range $-0.0752$ to $+0.0400$; 71 positive and 82 negative differences). For the raw baseline on Wine Quality Red, macro-F1 changed from 0.3478 to 0.2818 for random forest and from 0.3554 to 0.2802 for XGBoost. Across nine dataset--classifier matrices with complete applicable cells, 27 of 78 condition ranks changed; the mean absolute rank change was 0.62 and the maximum was six positions. These comparisons are descriptive across two split designs. Profile equality does not identify a shared person, specimen, protein, or document; the data files contain no such identity mapping. Full coverage, failures, and split assignments are recorded in the separate grouped-sensitivity evidence package.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The planned rank separation concerns cleaning behavior on three balanced datasets; it does not show that either method improves minority-class rebalancing. Elsewhere, the conclusions depend on which conditions apply, which runs complete, and how many dataset blocks support a comparison. Reporting coverage and failure denominators therefore changes how readers should interpret performance summaries, especially when methods do not share the same complete cells. The pair-specific raw-baseline tests remain exploratory, and the two-dataset mixed-family comparisons remain descriptive.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
Feature type also determines which conditions can be compared. Ordinary SMOTE-family conditions are unsupported for mixed datasets; mixed data uses SMOTENC before one-hot encoding. Because the hybrid cleaners receive ordinal-coded categorical columns, their cleaning distances can reflect arbitrary code order. The mixed family has a different condition matrix and only two dataset blocks, so pooling it with numeric results would conceal both the applicability rules and the limited inferential coverage.

The grouped-profile sensitivity addresses a different target: performance when predictor profiles in the test partition have not appeared in training. Its average change is modest, although Wine Quality Red shows larger raw-baseline decreases for random forest and XGBoost and other cells change in both directions. Equal profiles do not identify shared people or objects, and these results do not establish that row-wise evaluation is invalid.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
Sampling and runtime measurements describe costs associated with the evaluated conditions. Over-sampling generally increased training rows; under-sampling reduced them. Runtime varied by dataset and classifier, so these averages are useful for planning comparisons but do not guarantee a per-dataset cost. Practical choices also depend on class-wise performance, applicability, failure risk, and reproducibility.

\subsection{Ethical considerations and broader impact}

Two retained datasets come from sensitive health contexts. CMC is a subset of the 1987 National Indonesia Contraceptive Prevalence Survey, with demographic and socioeconomic predictors and contraceptive method as the outcome \citep{lim1999contraceptive}. Dermatology contains patient clinical and histopathological attributes; its UCI description reports that patient names and identification numbers were removed \citep{ilter1998dermatology}. This study reuses existing public records for aggregate methods benchmarking; it does not recruit participants or evaluate a model for deployment. Public availability alone does not establish consent for every downstream use or an ethics-board determination for a particular secondary analysis, and we do not infer either from the dataset licenses. The benchmark does not assess clinical validity, privacy risk, or subgroup fairness. Its results should not guide contraceptive, clinical, or other high-stakes decisions; any such use requires separate review of data authority and consent, privacy, subgroup performance, and applicable domain and institutional oversight.

\subsection{Limitations}

The retained 11 datasets are a protocol-defined subset, not a representative sample of every tabular domain. The mixed family has only two datasets, while the planned significant block contains three exactly balanced datasets and gives little resolution about severe imbalance. The ADASYN-excluded sensitivity adds numeric coverage but is post hoc, changes the condition set, and establishes neither equivalence nor a causal explanation. Imbalance strata are likewise descriptive and cannot support a method-selection rule.

Classifier and sampler settings are locked baselines rather than a tuning study, so different search budgets could alter performance and cost. Complete-cell filtering reduces the number of inferential blocks when methods fail or are inapplicable. On mixed data, ordinal-coded categories can impose artificial distances in hybrid cleaning. The grouped-profile sensitivity is post hoc, covers six datasets, changes the split unit, and has no identity mapping for interpreting equal profiles as the same entity.

The measurements and reproduction boundary also constrain interpretation. RSS values are endpoint process snapshots, not peak memory, and the original full-run training interpreter is unverified. Replaying frozen records reproduces analysis outputs but does not retrain benchmark models or provide an independent training replication. Independent human technical review remains outstanding.

\subsection{Reproducibility and future work}

The source and evidence package records the protocol, registry, acquisition provenance, executable benchmark and analysis scripts, focused tests, generated-figure definitions, and release validators. The analysis manifest records runtime package versions and SHA-256 hashes for its tables and figures; an analysis replay independently regenerates and compares those outputs. The supplement locates complete-cell coverage, class-wise recall, failure accounting, and the ADASYN-excluded sensitivity in named CSV files. Reprocessing the frozen result and failure records is an analysis-only reproduction; it does not rerun model training or constitute an independent replication. Future work could enlarge the retained dataset collection, implement categorical-only handling, add a separately timestamped protocol for tuning budgets, and test whether the observed conditionality persists under other model families. Such extensions would require a new scope or protocol amendment rather than being folded into this result set.

The separate grouped-profile sensitivity is documented in \path{docs/duplicate-profile-sensitivity-results.md}; its hash-verified output directory contains the split assignments, valid and failure records, cell summaries, rank comparisons, and run manifest. This post hoc grouped split design is separate from the analysis-only replay of the frozen row-wise records.

\section{Conclusion}

We compared fixed imbalance-handling strategies across 11 multiclass tabular datasets and report performance alongside applicability, sampler failures, complete-cell coverage, and computational cost. The planned XGBoost rank separation concerns two cleaning methods on three exactly balanced datasets; the post hoc nine-dataset sensitivity found no Holm-adjusted raw-baseline difference. Interpretation remains tied to the retained data and declared statistical resolution. Replaying frozen records reproduces analysis outputs, but an independent full model-training replication has not been established.
